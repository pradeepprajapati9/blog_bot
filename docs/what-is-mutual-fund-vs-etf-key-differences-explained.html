What is Mutual Fund vs ETF? Key Differences Explained

15 Aug 2026

When exploring options for growing your money, you've likely come across terms like Mutual Funds and Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs). Both are popular investment avenues in India, offering ways to diversify your portfolio without buying individual stocks. However, despite their similarities in pooling money from multiple investors to invest in a basket of securities, they operate quite differently. Understanding these key distinctions is crucial for making informed decisions tailored to your financial goals. This guide will break down what each is and highlight their main differences in simple terms.

Understanding Mutual Funds

A mutual fund is a professionally managed investment vehicle that pools money from many investors to purchase securities like stocks, bonds, or other assets. A fund manager actively or passively manages this pool, making investment decisions based on the fund's stated objective. When you invest in a mutual fund, you buy units, and the value of these units is determined by the fund's Net Asset Value (NAV). The NAV is calculated at the end of each trading day, reflecting the total value of the fund's assets minus its liabilities, divided by the number of outstanding units. You can typically invest in mutual funds directly through an Asset Management Company (AMC) or through distributors, often via Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) or lump-sum investments. They offer diversification and professional management, making them popular for long-term wealth creation.

Understanding Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs)

An Exchange Traded Fund (ETF) is also a basket of securities, but it trades like a regular stock on a stock exchange. Most ETFs are designed to track a specific index, such as the Nifty 50 or S&P 500, or commodities like gold. Unlike mutual funds, whose prices are determined once a day at NAV, ETFs can be bought and sold throughout the trading day at market-determined prices, which can fluctuate just like stock prices. To invest in an ETF, you need a demat account and a trading account, similar to buying stocks. ETFs are known for their transparency, often lower expense ratios compared to actively managed mutual funds, and flexibility due to their intraday tradability.

Key Difference 1: Trading and Pricing

One of the most significant differences between mutual funds and ETFs lies in how they are bought, sold, and priced. Mutual fund units are purchased or redeemed directly from the fund house at the day's closing Net Asset Value (NAV). This means you don't know the exact price until the end of the trading day. Transactions happen once per day. On the other hand, ETFs trade on stock exchanges in real-time, much like individual stocks. Their prices fluctuate throughout the trading day based on supply and demand, allowing investors to buy or sell them at any point during market hours. This requires a demat account, which is not strictly necessary for all mutual fund investments if you invest directly through the AMC via a folio.

Key Difference 2: Management Style and Costs

The management approach and associated costs also differ. Many mutual funds, especially equity-oriented ones, are actively managed. This means a fund manager actively researches, buys, and sells securities in an attempt to outperform a benchmark index. Active management often comes with higher expense ratios (the annual fee charged to cover operational expenses). While there are passively managed mutual funds (index funds) that mimic an index, most ETFs are passively managed. They aim to replicate the performance of a specific index rather than outperform it. This passive approach generally results in significantly lower expense ratios for ETFs compared to actively managed mutual funds. However, remember that you also pay brokerage charges when buying or selling ETFs, similar to stocks.

Key Difference 3: Liquidity and Flexibility

ETFs generally offer higher liquidity and flexibility compared to mutual funds. Since ETFs trade on an exchange, you can buy and sell units throughout the trading day, giving you immediate access to your funds (once the trade is settled). This allows for intraday trading strategies or quick adjustments to your portfolio if needed. Mutual funds, however, are typically less liquid. When you place a redemption request for a mutual fund, it is processed at the day's closing NAV, and the funds are usually credited to your bank account within a few business days (T+2 or T+3, where T is the transaction day). This makes mutual funds more suitable for long-term, 'buy-and-hold' investors who don't need frequent access to their capital.

Investment Accessibility and Suitability

Accessibility and suitability often play a role in choosing between the two. Mutual funds are generally considered more beginner-friendly, especially with the popularity of Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) that allow you to invest small, fixed amounts regularly. They are accessible even without a trading and demat account if you invest directly with the AMC. ETFs, by requiring a demat and trading account, involve a slightly higher barrier to entry for new investors. However, for those comfortable with stock market trading and seeking lower costs, market transparency, and intraday flexibility, ETFs can be an excellent choice. Both offer diversification, but the choice often comes down to your trading comfort, investment horizon, and cost sensitivity.

Conclusion

Both mutual funds and ETFs serve as excellent tools for diversification and wealth creation, but they cater to slightly different investor preferences and needs. Mutual funds typically offer professional management and are ideal for long-term, passive investors who prefer a hands-off approach and regular investments through SIPs. ETFs, with their real-time trading and generally lower expense ratios, appeal to those comfortable with stock market mechanics, who value intraday liquidity and direct control over their transactions. Before making a decision, understand your own investment goals, risk tolerance, and how much involvement you want in managing your portfolio. Consulting freely available educational resources and understanding the basics is always a good first step.
